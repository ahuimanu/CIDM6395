Guide · The proposal

The proposal

The proposal is the first document of your final project and the one I approve before you begin to build. Because we do not meet to review designs, it has to make the whole case for your project on the page: the problem, the synthesis prototype you will deliver this term, the full project it is a sample of, and the data and tools it depends on.

The nine sections

Write the proposal in proposal/proposal.md, in nine sections and no more than two pages of text, with your drawing of the project embedded in it. The sections follow the order in which a reviewer needs the information, and each one answers a single question. The proposal assignment in WTClass gives the full instructions for each section.

SectionWhat it answers
1. PretextWho has the problem, and what decision or operation does the project support?
2. Process flowIn what order do the areas act, and what breaks without each one?
3. The synthesis prototypeWhat exactly will run at the end of the term?
4. The full projectWhat does about a year of further work add?
5. DataWho answers for the data, and does it meet the four conditions?
6. Tools and platformWhat does the prototype run on, and does anything need my approval?
7. Risks and the first milestoneWhat is most likely to stop you, and what will you finish first?
8. Evidence you build onWhich appraisals and artifacts support the project, by file path?
9. AI useWhich AI tools will you use, and for what?

The pretext

As used in this course, the pretext is the problem your project serves, stated as a person or organization and the decision they would make with your result. "Analyzing housing data" is not a pretext, since no one acts on it. "A county appraisal office wants to know which neighborhoods its models undervalue, so that it can schedule field reviews" is one, because it identifies who acts and what they do. The organization may be real, or it may be a credible frame placed on real data.

The process flow

The process flow lists the areas in the order in which they act, and for each area in the prototype it states what would break if that area were removed. That second statement is the test of integration. An area whose removal breaks nothing is decoration, and it does not count toward the requirement that the prototype integrate SS and at least two of BA, DM, and CN.

The synthesis prototype

Section 3 describes what will run at the end of the term: the inputs (which data, how many records, over what period), what each area does to them, the outputs, the interface, and the one complete path from the source data to what a user sees. Write it precisely enough that you and I can both check, at the end of the term, whether you built it, because that is the comparison the final review makes.

The drawing

The drawing follows the form shown in Figure 03: the pretext on one line at the top, a box for each area in flow order with arrows between them, a solid box around the synthesis prototype, and dashed boxes around what the full project adds. Draw your own project. A relabeled copy of an orientation model does not show me how your project is designed.

Data and tools

Section 5 gives a table of your data sources (the source, who answers for it, what one record describes, how you obtain it, and its terms) followed by a sentence or two on each of the four data conditions that the prototype guide explains. Section 6 lists the Python version, the main libraries, and any other tools. I approve tools other than Python only when they run on Windows, macOS, and Linux, so that I can run your prototype on any of them.

After you submit

I review the proposal and commit feedback/proposal.md, which holds my decision and a set of numbered questions; you answer the questions in proposal/consultation-response.md. The proposal cycle describes each step. You may change the project after it is approved, provided you explain the change in your root README, since the final review compares what you deliver with what you proposed.
