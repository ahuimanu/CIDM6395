The Proposal

The proposal turns your project idea into a written commitment that I can approve. It has nine sections and one drawing. This guide explains what each part must show, and why the proposal is written so that it can replace a design meeting.

What the proposal is for

The proposal does two jobs. First, it lets me decide whether the project is feasible before you spend the term building it. Second, it fixes what you promise to deliver, so that at the end of the term you and I can both tell whether you delivered it. A proposal that is vague on either point fails both jobs.

The proposal also replaces a design meeting. Everything I would ask across a table, the proposal must answer on the page. When it does not, I ask in writing and you answer in writing. That exchange is the proposal cycle.

The nine sections

The sections follow the order in which a reviewer needs the information. Each one answers a single question.

SectionThe question it answers
1. PretextWho has the problem, and what decision or operation does the project support?
2. Process flowIn what order do the areas act, and what breaks without each one?
3. The synthesis prototypeWhat exactly will run at the end of the term?
4. The full projectWhat does about one year of further work add?
5. DataWho answers for the data, and does it meet the four conditions?
6. Tools and platformWhat does the prototype run on, and does anything need approval?
7. Risks and the first milestoneWhat is most likely to stop you, and what will you finish first?
8. Evidence you build onWhich appraisals and artifacts support this project, by file path?
9. AI useWhich AI tools will you use, and for what?

The three sections that decide most proposals

The pretext

A good pretext passes one test: you can identify the person who would act on the result, and the action they would take. "Analyzing housing data" fails the test, because no one acts on it. "A county appraisal office wants to know which neighborhoods its models undervalue, so that it can schedule field reviews" passes. The pretext belongs to a user or an organization, real or a credible frame on real data. It does not belong to the course, so it never says that the project demonstrates synthesis.

The process flow

The process flow lists the areas in the order they act. For each area in the prototype, it states what breaks if you remove that area. This is the test of integration. If nothing breaks, the area is decoration, and it does not count. The prototype must integrate SS and at least two of the other three areas. SS is required because the prototype must function, and software is what runs.

The synthesis prototype

The synthesis prototype is the part of the full project that runs at the end of the term. It carries real data along one complete path, from the source to an output a user sees. The section states the inputs, the processing in each area, the outputs, the interface, and the path. Write it so precisely that a stranger could check, at the end of the term, whether you delivered it. Figure 03 shows how to mark it on your drawing.

The drawing

The proposal includes your own drawing of the project, in the form of Figure 03: the pretext in one line above, a box for each area in flow order, arrows, a solid box around the synthesis prototype, and dashed boxes around what the full project adds. Draw your project, not one of the orientation models with new labels. A copied model shows only that you saw the model.

Data and tools

Section 5 holds the data to four conditions: relevance, accountability, availability, and quality. The prototype guide explains each one. Most proposals that fail on data use a sample dataset from a site such as Kaggle, where someone else framed the problem and cleaned the data. Public data from an agency that answers for it is the usual remedy.

Section 6 lists what the prototype runs on. The prototype is written in Python. Any other language or scripting tool needs my approval in the proposal cycle, and I approve only tools that run on Windows, macOS, and Linux.

Changing course later

You may change the project after the proposal. Explain the change in your root README. An explained change costs nothing, because a better project is the point. An unexplained change makes the final work hard to reconcile with what was approved, and that costs continuity.
