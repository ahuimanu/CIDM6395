Guide · The proposal

The proposal

The proposal is where you commit, in writing, to what you will build. I read it to decide whether the project is feasible, and at the end of the term we both read it again to see whether you built what you said you would.

Instead of a meeting

We do not hold design meetings, so the proposal has to answer on the page the questions I would otherwise ask across a table. Where it does not, I ask in writing and you answer in writing. The proposal cycle sets out how that exchange works.

The nine sections

The sections come in the order a reviewer needs them, and each answers one question.

SectionThe question it answers
1. PretextWho has the problem, and what decision or operation does the project support?
2. Process flowIn what order do the areas act, and what breaks without each one?
3. The synthesis prototypeWhat exactly will run at the end of the term?
4. The full projectWhat does about a year of further work add?
5. DataWho answers for the data, and does it meet the four conditions?
6. Tools and platformWhat does the prototype run on, and does anything need my approval?
7. Risks and the first milestoneWhat is most likely to stop you, and what will you finish first?
8. Evidence you build onWhich appraisals and artifacts support the project, by file path?
9. AI useWhich AI tools will you use, and for what?

Pretext, flow, and prototype

Three sections need the most care. The pretext passes if I can identify the person who would act on your result and the action they would take. "Analyzing housing data" fails, because nobody acts on it; "a county appraisal office wants to know which neighborhoods its models undervalue, so it can schedule field reviews" passes. The pretext belongs to a user or an organization, real or a credible frame on real data, and never to the course.

The process flow lists the areas in the order they act and says, for each one in the prototype, what would break without it. If nothing would break, the area is decoration and does not count toward the requirement of SS plus two others.

Section 3 describes the synthesis prototype: its inputs, what each area does to them, its outputs, its interface, and the one complete path from source to result. Write it precisely enough that a stranger could check, at the end of the term, whether you delivered it. Figure 03 shows how to mark it on your drawing.

The drawing

Include your own drawing of the project in the form shown in Figure 03: the pretext on one line above, a box for each area in flow order with arrows between them, a solid box around the prototype, and dashed boxes around what the full project adds. Draw your own project. An orientation model with new labels tells me only that you saw the model.

Data and tools

In section 5, the data have to meet four conditions (relevance, accountability, availability, and quality), which the prototype guide explains. A sample dataset from a site like Kaggle usually fails at least two of them; public data from an agency that stands behind it usually passes. Section 6 lists the tools. The prototype is in Python, and anything else needs my approval, which I give only for tools that run on Windows, macOS, and Linux.

Changing course

You may change the project after the proposal. Explain the change in your root README and it costs nothing. An unexplained change leaves the final work hard to reconcile with what I approved, and that costs continuity points.
