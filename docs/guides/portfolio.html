Guide · The portfolio and bio

The portfolio and bio

Your portfolio records what the program has prepared you to do. It sits in the portfolio/ folder of your repository and holds your bio, your skills inventory, and your four area appraisals, each with the work it cites. Because the proposal, the paper, and the debrief all draw on it, it comes before the project.

What the portfolio contains

By the final commit, portfolio/ holds these files and folders:

  1. BIO.md, your professional bio.
  2. inventory/README.md, the skills inventory you wrote for Evolution 0.
  3. BA/, DM/, SS/, and CN/, each holding your final appraisal for that area as README.md, together with the work the appraisal cites.

During the term you wrote the inventory and the appraisals in top-level folders named evolution-0 to evolution-4. Move them into portfolio/ before the final commit, following the table in Your repository, so that every repository has the same layout and I can check each one in the same way.

The bio

Write the bio for a reader outside the course, such as a prospective employer, since the portfolio is the part of your repository that such a reader opens first. It should say who you are, what you did in the program and in your work, and what you intend to do next. Keep it specific: mention the courses, projects, and roles that matter, rather than describing yourself in general terms.

The appraisals

Each appraisal assesses where you stand in one area. As used in this course, knowledge is what you know about an area and a skill is what you can do with it, and an appraisal has to show skills, proven on work you actually did, rather than list knowledge. "SQL" is knowledge; "writing a windowed query to rank customers by quarter" is a skill.

An appraisal has three parts. The assignment pages in WTClass give the full prompt for each.

  1. A self-assessment in three tiers: what you can do again without help, where you are weak in ways that would matter for the capstone and why, and what practitioners in the area do that your courses did not cover. The third tier requires some reading about the field.
  2. Support for those claims: links to your own work in the repository, the sources you learned from (titles, authors, URLs), and a short statement of what you can now do in the area.
  3. The area's place in the capstone: how it connects to the other three areas, in general and in your own work, and a statement of your own about how the four fit together.

Evidence

The work an appraisal cites has to be in the repository. A link to another repository or to a cloud drive can accompany the work but cannot replace it, because I evaluate the repository as it stands at the deadline, and by then a link may have changed or stopped working. A claim that points to no file in the repository gives me nothing to check, so it does not count as evidence.

The evolution rubric records the three problems that cost the most points in past terms: an integration section copied from one appraisal to the next with only the area name changed, a strength claimed with no work behind it, and work that was never committed to the course repository.

How the rest of the capstone uses it

The proposal cites your appraisals by file path in section 8, and the paper uses them as evidence for what it says about each area. In the debrief I ask about the work they cite.
