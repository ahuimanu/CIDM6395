The Portfolio, the Bio, and the Four Appraisals

The portfolio and bio are demonstrated competency. Together they show how the work you completed in the program is the foundation you need to deliver synthesized value. The portfolio is a folder for each of the four areas, each holding an appraisal and the work that proves it. The bio introduces the person who did the work. This guide explains what each one is, why they come first, and what an appraisal must argue.

What the portfolio is

As used in this course, the portfolio lives in your private GitHub repository in the course organization. The repository has two jobs at once. It is where you submit every piece of the capstone, and it is the record of your work that you can show after the course ends.

The portfolio is the portfolio/ folder of your repository. It holds the bio, the skills inventory from Evolution 0, and four area folders, one each for BA, DM, SS, and CN. Each area folder holds three things:

  1. A README that explains what is in the folder and which file is final.
  2. Your final appraisal for that area (Evolution 1 for BA, 2 for DM, 3 for SS, 4 for CN).
  3. The actual work the appraisal cites: code, notebooks, SQL, schemas, diagrams, configurations, reports.

The four folders correspond to the four regions of Figure 01. Each appraisal describes one region as it exists in your own education. The prototype then sits where regions overlap.

The bio

The bio is portfolio/BIO.md, linked from the root README. It states who you are, the work you did in the program and outside it, and what you intend to do with it. The appraisals show competency area by area. The bio states the professional who holds those competencies, and it is the first thing a reader outside the course will open.

Why the portfolio comes first

A synthesis integrates parts that already exist. Before you can integrate four areas, you have to know what you can do in each one, and where you cannot yet do enough. The appraisals establish that. For this reason the evolutions come before the proposal, and the proposal builds on them.

The appraisals are used again at each later stage:

StageHow it uses the appraisals
Evolution 0 (inventory)Lists what you know and can do, course by course. Each appraisal elaborates two rows of it.
Evolutions 1 to 4One appraisal per area: strengths, weaknesses, blind spots, evidence, and how the area fits with the other three.
Proposal, section 8Cites the appraisals and artifacts the project builds on, by file path, and identifies one weakness the project will make you work on.
Synthesis paperRefers to the appraisals and artifacts to support its claims about each area.
DebriefTests whether the capability you claimed matches the work you can explain.

What an appraisal argues

Each appraisal uses the competency model from orientation. Knowledge is what you know. A skill is how you use it. The pair, proven against a task you actually did, is a competency. "SQL" is knowledge. "Writing a windowed query to rank customers by quarter" is a skill. An appraisal that lists knowledge and calls it competence has not yet argued anything.

The appraisal makes its argument in three parts.

1. Three tiers of self-assessment

  1. What you know. Competencies you could use again tomorrow without help, each grounded in a course or project.
  2. Where you are weak. Specific gaps that would matter for the capstone, and why each is a gap: little practice, a hard concept, a tool you never used.
  3. What you wish you knew, or do not know you are missing. What practitioners in the area do that your courses did not reach. This tier requires some research into the field. It is not the second tier reworded.

2. Support

Links to your own work in the repository, the sources you learned from (titles, authors, URLs), and a short capability statement: what you can do in the area, whether you could do it outside a classroom, and how ready you are to use it in the prototype.

3. Contribution toward the capstone

Why the area can integrate with the other three, how it integrates (the concrete mechanisms), how it has integrated with your own prior work, and a new statement of your own about how the four areas fit together. By Evolution 4, that statement is the first draft of your capstone thesis.

Evidence is a file, not a link

The work an appraisal cites has to be in the repository. A link to another repository, a cloud drive, or a course site can supplement the work. It does not replace it, because a link can break, move, or change after the deadline, and I evaluate the repository as it stands at the deadline. A claim with no file behind it cannot be checked, and an unchecked claim does not count as evidence.

Three common failures

Past semesters show three failures that cost the most:

  1. An integration section copied from the previous appraisal with the area name changed. Each area depends on the others in a different way, and the section has to say how.
  2. A strength with no artifact behind it. If I cannot open the work, I cannot verify the claim.
  3. Work that is not in the course repository by the due date. Work that is not there cannot be evaluated.

A student may object, fairly, that a weakness stated plainly looks bad in a portfolio meant to be shown. It does not, for a reason the field already accepts: an engineer who states the limits of a design is trusted more than one who states none. A calibrated weakness in an appraisal does the same work.

Files in your repository

These files stay in your course repository. Folder and file names can vary if your root README says where each one is. The repository guide shows the whole structure.

File or folderWhat it holds
README.mdThe root README: what the portfolio contains, where each item lives, and a link to the bio.
