The portfolio and bio

The portfolio is the evidence that your coursework prepared you for this project: what you can do in each of the four areas, shown with work you actually did. The bio puts a person in front of that evidence.

What is in it

The portfolio is the portfolio/ folder of your private repository. It holds your bio, the skills inventory you wrote for Evolution 0, and one folder for each area, BA, DM, SS, and CN. Each area folder holds your final appraisal for that area as README.md, next to the code, notebooks, SQL, schemas, diagrams, and reports the appraisal cites. The four folders line up with the four regions of Figure 01; the prototype sits where the regions overlap.

Your repository does two jobs. It is how you submit the capstone, and it is the record you can show an employer when the course is over. The portfolio is the part an employer will read first, which is one reason the bio belongs in it.

The bio

portfolio/BIO.md says who you are, what you did in the program and outside it, and what you mean to do next. Keep it professional and specific. The appraisals show your competency area by area; the bio says whose competency it is.

Why it comes before the proposal

You can only integrate what you already have. Before you propose a project that joins four areas, you need to know what you can do in each one and where you fall short, and the appraisals are where you work that out. The proposal then cites them by file path in section 8, the paper uses them as evidence for its claims about each area, and the debrief tests whether what you claimed matches what you can explain.

What an appraisal argues

Each appraisal rests on the competency model from orientation. Knowledge is what you know; a skill is how you use it; and the two together, proven on a task you actually did, make a competency. "SQL" is knowledge. "Writing a windowed query to rank customers by quarter" is a skill. An appraisal that lists knowledge and calls it competence has not yet made an argument.

The appraisal starts with three tiers of self-assessment: what you can do again tomorrow without help, where you are weak in ways that would matter for the capstone (and why), and what practitioners in the area do that your courses never reached. That third tier takes some reading about the field, and it is the one most often skipped or folded into the second.

It then backs those claims with links to your own work in the repository, the sources you learned from (titles, authors, URLs), and a short capability statement. It ends with the area's place in the capstone: why and how it connects to the other three, where it already has in your own work, and a statement of your own about how the four fit together. By Evolution 4 that statement is the first draft of your thesis.

Evidence

The work an appraisal cites has to be in the repository. A link to another repository or a cloud drive can sit beside the work but cannot stand in for it, since links break and I evaluate the repository as it is at the deadline. A claim I cannot open a file to check does not count as evidence.

Three things have cost students the most here. One is an integration section copied from the previous appraisal with the area name changed, when each area depends on the others differently. Another is a strength with no work behind it. The third is work that was never committed to the course repository, which leaves me nothing to evaluate.

Weaknesses belong in a portfolio you intend to show. An engineer who states the limits of a design is trusted more than one who claims it has none, and a reader of your appraisal will make the same judgment.
